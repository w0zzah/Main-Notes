EIGENVALUES and EIGENVECTORS

Ax transforms x to another vector, which could be longer or shorter, and could be in
a different direction. The eigenvectors of A are the vectors whose direction is preserved
under this transformation.
The eigenvectors of a square matrix A are the non-zero vectors v that remain proportional
to v when they are multiplied by A. In other words, they are the v ̸= 0 for which
Av = λv

(1)

where λ, a scalar, is called the eigenvalue associated with the eigenvector v.
You will note that the eigenvectors and eigenvalues “belong” to the square matrix A – we
speak of “the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of A”. The German word for “characteristic”
or “own” is “eigen”.
In (1) both λ and v are unknowns. Finding these unknowns for a given matrix A is
called solving an eigen problem1 . Other non-matrix problems in which the unknowns also
depend on an unknown parameter are sometimes referred to as eigenproblems.

So we see that by definition an eigenvector v of a matrix A is a vector whose direction is
unchanged by the transformation A; it is only stretched or compressed, and the eigenvalue
gives the stretch.
The general method to solve Av = λv for v and λ is as follows.
Av = λv ⇐⇒ Av − λv = (A − λI)v = 0,
where I is the identity matrix. Now we have already said that v ̸= 0. Imagine that our
new matrix A − λI had an inverse, call it B. Then if we multiplied both sides of our
equation by B (on the left) then on the LHS we would get B(A − λI)v = v while on
the RHS we would get B0 = 0. Since the LHS equals the RHS this tells us that v = 0,
which we know it can’t be. So we have found that if (A − λI) has an inverse then we get
a contradiction. Therefore (A − λI) can’t have an inverse – it must be singular. Recall
that a singular matrix is one whose determinant is zero. Hence the strategy to find the
eigenvalues and eigenvectors of A is

1. Solve det (A − λI) = 0 for the eigenvalues λ.
2. Then solve (A − λI)v = 0 for the eigenvectors v.
